# The brief

Write the brief before you change anything. It is short, it comes from the product in front
of you, and every pass follows it. Take it from what the screen says the product does and
who uses it, never from what is in fashion.

## The six lines

1. **Job.** Who uses the screen and what they come to do, in one plain sentence.
   "Owners of small design studios check which invoices are paid and send reminders."
2. **Tone.** Three concrete words for how the product should feel. Do not use clean, modern,
   sleek, minimal, premium or beautiful: they describe every template and decide nothing.
   Good words make you reject options: "calm, exact, warm", "fast, technical, dry".
3. **Setting.** The place, the material or the time of day the product belongs to. An
   invoicing tool lives near paper, ink and a desk lamp; an on-call tool near night shifts
   and instrument panels; a gardening app near soil and morning light. The setting chooses
   the tint of the neutrals, the accent and the imagery.
4. **Focus.** The one or two places on the screen that get rich color and detail. Take them
   from the product's own content: the main chart, the document the product produces, a
   physical object it deals with (a card, a parcel, a plant). Everything else stays quiet.
5. **Mark.** One small detail that only this product would have, used the same way
   everywhere it fits: a paper-edge on documents, keycaps for shortcuts, a live dot for
   real-time data. One detail, not five.
6. **Keep.** What must not change: the logo, a real brand color or typeface, the section
   order, required legal text, the accessibility target (WCAG AA by default).

The examples here show the shape of an answer. Derive yours from the product, even when an
example looks close.

## Worked example 1: an invoicing dashboard

The before: a violet gradient shell, emoji navigation, glowing stat cards, "Welcome back,
Sam! 👋", "Supercharge your invoicing with AI-powered magic!!".

- Job: owners of small studios see what is paid, what is late, and send reminders.
- Tone: calm, exact, a little warm.
- Setting: paper and ink at a desk; a ledger.
- Focus: the outstanding amount as a large figure, and the list of late invoices.
- Mark: amounts set in a mono face with tabular figures, like a ledger column.
- Keep: the name Ledgerly, the four navigation sections, the stat cards' content.

What follows from it: neutrals with a faint warm tint; one ink-blue accent for the primary
action and the selected row; the headline becomes the month; the hype line becomes what the
page shows; the stat cards become numbers with a comparison ("+12% vs September").

## Worked example 2: a status page for an API

The before: a black page with neon green borders, a pulsing dot on every service, uppercase
tracked headings, "⚡ Blazing fast uptime ⚡".

- Job: developers check if the API is up and read about current incidents.
- Tone: plain, precise, reassuring.
- Setting: an instrument panel at night.
- Focus: the 90-day uptime bars of each service; the open incident, if there is one.
- Mark: a single live dot next to "Updated 12 s ago", nowhere else.
- Keep: the service list, the incident history format, the subscribe button.

What follows from it: dark graphite surfaces; green, amber and red only for status; no pulse
except the one live dot (inside a `role="status"` region); headings in sentence case; the
hero line becomes "All systems operational" or the open incident.

## Show it

Show the user the audit summary and the brief together, then wait. Name the claims that need
a source in the same message, so the user can confirm or remove them in one answer.
